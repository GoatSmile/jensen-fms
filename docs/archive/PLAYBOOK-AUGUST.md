## The one thing I would most like you to try: calls that become tickets

This is the biggest change since you last looked at the app, and it makes far
more sense after doing it once than after reading about it.

**What it does.** Someone rings the workshop number. The app answers, plays a
short "this call is recorded" notice in Danish and English, then rings a real
mobile. If you pick up, you have an ordinary conversation — and in the
background the app records it, transcribes it, works out which customer and
which bike it was about, and leaves a **draft ticket** waiting in **Inbox**. If
nobody answers within twenty seconds, the caller gets voicemail instead, and
that becomes a draft ticket too.

Nothing is ever sent to a customer, and no draft becomes a real ticket without a
person clicking. That is what "review only" means, and it stays that way this
month regardless of what you do below.

Right now the phone that rings is my test mobile. **It would be far more useful
if it rang yours** — then the whole thing is being tested on your voice, your
customers' names and your Danish, which is the one part I cannot test on my own.

### Trying it end to end — about five minutes

You need two phones: the one that should ring, and any other phone to call from.

1. **Admin → Settings → Calls & inbound.**
2. Under **When someone calls** it should already say *Ring a phone, then
   voicemail*. Leave that as it is.
3. In **Phone to ring**, replace my number with your own mobile, written in
   international form — **+45** followed by your number, no spaces. Save.
4. From the *other* phone, call **+1 762 500 0850**. It is an American test
   number, so your phone will treat it as an international call — a few kroner.
   Your real Danish number takes its place before go-live.
5. You will hear the recorded-call notice, and then your mobile rings. Answer
   it.
6. Talk for thirty seconds or so, the way a customer would: a name, a bike,
   something wrong with it. In Danish, ideally.
7. Hang up both ends.
8. Give it a minute, then open **Work → Inbox**. Your call should be sitting
   there — the recording, the transcript, and what the app made of it.

**What is worth looking at when it lands.** Did it get the words right? Did it
work out who was calling? Did it find the bike? Did it understand what the
customer actually wanted? Anything it got wrong is worth a line in your notes;
that is precisely the feedback that improves it.

**One thing worth knowing in advance:** every Danish test so far has been a
voicemail, and voicemail audio is poor. A two-way call is much cleaner, so I
expect better — but a bridged Danish call is honestly the one test I have not
been able to run. If the transcript comes back as nonsense, you have found
something real rather than broken something.

**When you want your phone out of it again**, put my number
(+45 50 36 71 11) back in the same field, or set **When someone calls** to
*Take a voicemail*. Nothing else is affected either way.

To be clear, none of this is one of the two switches further down — changing
which phone rings is reversible in a second, and review-only mode stays on
throughout.

## A suggested hour, if you'd like a route

No obligation — but this order touches most of the app and follows roughly how
a real week runs.

1. **Dashboard.** What needs you today. Money at the top, then your pipelines.
   If a card is not there, nothing needs you.
2. **A bike.** Bikes → pick one → read its page. History, parts, photos, its QR
   code, who owns it.
3. **The workshop floor, on your phone.** **Work → Workshop floor** on your
   actual phone, not the computer. That is how your mechanics will meet this
   app, and it is built for gloves and bad light. **Scan** is worth a try too.
4. **A repair, start to finish.** Work → Tickets → New ticket against a real
   bike. Then "Start work order", add a couple of parts, mark it done. The
   ticket closes itself and the job appears as ready to invoice.
5. **A build.** Orders → Manufacturing → pick an order → open a bike → Build.
   This is where a bike gets assembled: confirm the frame number, pull in the
   recipe, finish the build. Stock comes off automatically.
6. **Print something.** A purchase order, a picking list, a sheet of QR
   stickers. These are the paper your shop actually runs on — they should look
   right coming off the printer.
7. **Your customers on a map.** Customers → Map. Where your bikes are, whose
   agreements are running out.

## Where your eye is worth more than mine

If you have appetite beyond exploring, these are the places where only you can
tell whether the app is right. All four are *checking*, not typing.

- **The bike recipes.** Bikes → Bike templates → open one. Is that genuinely
  what goes into that bike, in those quantities? These lists drive costing,
  purchasing and picking, so an error here spreads everywhere.
- **Your painter's prices.** Admin → Services. Your real price lists, including
  the quantity breaks. Do the numbers match what Metacoat actually charges?
- **Part names and categories.** Parts → All parts. Anything named confusingly,
  filed in the wrong category, or duplicated?
- **Stock figures will look wrong, and that is expected.** Opening stock was
  never entered, so some parts show odd or negative numbers — you will see one
  part sitting at minus 207. That is not a fault; the app only knows what it has
  watched come in and go out. One physical count on the morning we go live fixes
  it, not typing.

## What is new since you last used it

In plain terms:

- **The whole app speaks Danish**, and now so does every last screen.
- **It looks like Jensen** rather than a generic admin tool. Six colours with
  fixed meanings — blue for navigation and main actions, sand/gold for money and
  "worth a look", green for ready and on schedule, red only for something
  genuinely wrong, rust for buying and suppliers, purple for settings and
  agreements. Red is deliberately rare, so if you see it, it means you.
- **The left menu is seven groups instead of a long list**, grouped the way you
  talk about the job. Open a group and it stays how you left it.
- **All the drop-down lists live on one page**: Admin → Lists. Categories,
  colours, coatings, customer segments, bike families, HS codes, stock
  locations, paint part types — tabs down the side. Old bookmarks still work;
  they just bring you here.
- **Phone calls become tickets** — recorded, transcribed, matched to a customer
  and left as a draft in **Inbox**. The section above is the one worth five
  minutes of your own phone.
- **Paint became a general "services" model** with your price lists and quantity
  breaks, so washing or priming can join later without rebuilding.
- **People & roles** — each role can eventually get its own login showing only
  their part of the app. Built, not switched on. Something to do with the team
  once you have seen it.
- **The dashboard got the money band and trend charts.**

Nothing moved address. Every bookmark you have still lands where it did.

I also spent a full day testing the app end to end — every screen, and every job

## Your own copy, on your own hardware

Something I would like to propose rather than leave for you to ask about.

The idea is simple: once a month, a complete copy of the system — the database,
the photos, the call recordings, the code and the documentation — lands as a
single encrypted file on a server of yours. The password lives in your own
password manager, never next to the file itself. Not because I expect anything
to go wrong, but because a business should not sit on one person's laptop, and
that includes mine.

The part worth sitting down over is the difference between *having* a copy and
being able to *use* one. Rather than assure you it works, I would suggest we
spend half an hour doing it for real on your machine: unpack an archive, load
the database into a fresh test project, open the app against it. Then you have
seen it work rather than taken my word for it. That is a natural fit for the
day at my place, below.

**The one part I cannot decide on my own** is where the copy should live. Is
there a server or NAS at the workshop that would suit, and who looks after it?
If there isn't one, that is no obstacle — there are other routes, and it is a
small purchase rather than a project. Either way it is worth five minutes of
your thinking before I build the routine around the wrong assumption.
